Pushing the boundaries of actuators-to-task kinematic inversion: from fully actuated to underactuated (soft) robots

Inverse kinematics for robots that cannot hold every pose.

Conventional closed-loop inverse kinematics assumes that actuators can maintain arbitrary robot configurations. We extend this approach to underactuated robots, such as soft arms, tendon-driven hands, and balancing humanoids, by introducing two algorithms that solve arbitrary tasks by computing configurations the robot can physically maintain.

Fewer actuators than degrees of freedom

The robots we have in mind

Explainer 1 · 1.5 min

The problem

With a motor at every joint, inverse kinematics is essentially a geometric problem: any configuration that reaches the target can, in principle, be maintained by the motors. Replace two motors with a single tendon, however, and one direction of motion remains unactuated. Along this direction, the equilibrium is determined by gravity and elastic forces. Consequently, most configurations that reach the target can no longer be maintained. The configurations that the robot can hold form an equilibrium manifold \(\mathcal{E}\), and any valid inverse-kinematics solution must lie within it.

What the paper contributes

Inverse kinematics on configurations the robot can hold

A general formulation

We extend closed-loop inverse kinematics to underactuated mechanical systems by restricting solutions to the set of configurations the robot can maintain in static equilibrium. Classical IK is recovered as the fully actuated special case, while actuator-space inversion in soft robotics corresponds to a flat equilibrium set.

Algorithm 1

When the passive coordinates can be determined by solving the statics in advance, we formulate CLIK directly on the equilibrium set and establish conditions for exponential convergence.

Algorithm 2

We express static equilibrium as a holonomic constraint and incorporate it into the control loop as an additional task, following constrained CLIK. This avoids explicitly solving the statics while retaining formal convergence conditions.

Algorithm 1 · statics solved beforehand

Fix the actuators and the robot settles in a held configuration. Where \(H_{\mathrm{uu}}\) is invertible, the statics define a local branch \(q_{\mathrm u} = \bar q_{\mathrm u}(q_{\mathrm a})\): pick a stable one and solve it beforehand. The result is a map from actuators to task, defined on held configurations only, and classic closed-loop IK runs on it with Jacobian \(J = J_{\mathrm a} - J_{\mathrm u} H_{\mathrm{uu}}^{-1} H_{\mathrm{ua}}\): differentiating \(G_{\mathrm u}(q_{\mathrm a}, \bar q_{\mathrm u}(q_{\mathrm a})) = 0\) gives \(\partial \bar q_{\mathrm u}/\partial q_{\mathrm a} = -H_{\mathrm{uu}}^{-1}H_{\mathrm{ua}}\), and the chain rule does the rest.

\(\dot q_{\mathrm a} = J_{h\circ h_{\mathrm a}}^{-1}\,\Gamma\,\big(\bar x - h\circ h_{\mathrm a}(q_{\mathrm a})\big)\)

Needs
an explicit solution of the statics, \(\bar q_{\mathrm u}(q_{\mathrm a})\)
Gives
a light loop with one coordinate per actuator; every intermediate reference lies on \(\mathcal{E}\)

Algorithm 2 · statics solved online

When \(G_{\mathrm u}=0\) cannot be solved for \(q_{\mathrm u}\) in advance, the loop solves it online. It asks both the task error and \(G_{\mathrm u}\) to decay exponentially, so the statics hold asymptotically; at each step that gives a linear system in \((\dot q_{\mathrm a}, \dot q_{\mathrm u})\):

\(J_{\mathrm a}\dot q_{\mathrm a} + J_{\mathrm u}\dot q_{\mathrm u} = \Gamma_{\mathcal Z}(\bar x - h)\)
\(H_{\mathrm{ua}}\dot q_{\mathrm a} + H_{\mathrm{uu}}\dot q_{\mathrm u} = -\Gamma_{\mathcal E} G_{\mathrm u}\)

The projectors \(P_{\mathcal E}\), \(P_{\mathcal Z}\) write out its solution. Where \(\mathcal{E}\) meets the target set in several points, the loop reaches one of them, depending on where it starts.

\((\dot q_{\mathrm a}, \dot q_{\mathrm u}) = P_{\mathcal E} J_{\mathrm a}^{-1}\Gamma_{\mathcal Z}(\bar x - h) - P_{\mathcal Z} H_{\mathrm{uu}}^{-1}\Gamma_{\mathcal E} G_{\mathrm u}\)

Needs
gradients of \(h\) and \(G_{\mathrm u}\); \(J_{\mathrm a}\), \(H_{\mathrm{uu}}\) and \(I - J_{\mathrm a}^{-1}J_{\mathrm u}H_{\mathrm{uu}}^{-1}H_{\mathrm{ua}}\) invertible along the way (the last one: the actuators still move the task along \(\mathcal{E}\))
Gives
starts anywhere these inverses stay well defined, also off \(\mathcal{E}\); no case-by-case derivation; heavier per step

Side note

If you work in soft robotics

You may recognize the familiar picture of continuum robotics: actuator, configuration, and task spaces, with an actuation model connecting the first two. In this simplified representation, all three spaces have the same dimension, and the actuators fully determine the robot’s configuration.

In reality, the configuration space is much larger. The configurations the robot can maintain in equilibrium form a lower-dimensional set \(\mathcal{E}\), whose dimension matches the number of actuators. The mapping from actuation to task space is defined on \(\mathcal{E}\).

Interestingly, when gravity is neglected, elasticity is linear, stiffness is uniform within each actuated section, and the rest shape is straight, \(\mathcal{E}\) becomes exactly the flat surface assumed by the familiar representation. This helps explain why the fully actuated approximation has worked so well in practice.

Results

Simulations: a soft arm and a balancing dancer

Takeaway. Algorithm 2 approaches the target in every soft-arm experiment. For Algorithm 1, accuracy depends on the quality of the statics model: improving the model or introducing feedback reduces the tracking error. On the dancer, both algorithms track the reference, although a 20% model error introduces steady-state offsets. Unstable target configurations, instead, cannot be maintained.

We test both algorithms on both systems, in feedforward (ff) and feedback (fb) modes. In feedforward mode, the algorithm computes the task error using its own predicted state \(\bar q\); in feedback mode, it uses the measured robot configuration \(q\). A low-level controller tracks the commanded actuated coordinates \(\bar q_{\mathrm a}\), while the passive coordinates \(q_{\mathrm u}\) evolve according to the robot’s mechanics.

For Algorithm 1, we deliberately use a statics model that neglects gravity, to illustrate the consequences of this approximation, together with a Jacobian-transpose update \(\dot q_{\mathrm a} = J^\top \Gamma e\).

Practitioner's guide

Using the algorithms on your robot

1. Match the math to your robot

\(q\)
the full configuration: joint angles, curvatures, shape parameters
\(q_{\mathrm a}\)
actuated coordinates: motor angles, tendon lengths, chamber volumes
\(q_{\mathrm u}\)
what still moves if you block the actuators: passive joints, curvature differences within a segment
\(c(q)\)
the map \(q \mapsto (q_{\mathrm a}, q_{\mathrm u})\); in practice choose \(q_{\mathrm a}\) from the actuators and add enough \(q_{\mathrm u}\) to fix the shape
\(x = h(q)\)
the task: tip position, pose, centre of mass, contact point
\(G(q)\)
potential forces (elastic, gravitational): what remains of the dynamics after removing all terms in \(\ddot q\), \(\dot q\) and \(\tau\)
\(G_{\mathrm u}\)
the part of \(G\) along \(q_{\mathrm u}\): forces the actuators cannot counteract. Held configurations satisfy \(G_{\mathrm u}=0\)

2. Pick the strategy

Can you solve \(G_{\mathrm u}(q_{\mathrm a}, q_{\mathrm u}) = 0\) for \(q_{\mathrm u} = \bar q_{\mathrm u}(q_{\mathrm a})\), and evaluate it quickly?

  • Yes: Algorithm 1, statics solved beforehand. Simpler, cheaper per step, easier to tune.
  • No: Algorithm 2, statics solved online. More general and needs no case-by-case derivation, at the price of more computation and more sensitivity to poor conditioning.

3. Practical remarks

  • Near singularities, use a damped inverse in place of a plain one, with either strategy.
  • If \(c^{-1}\) is hard to derive symbolically, integrate \(\dot q\) from \((\dot q_{\mathrm a}, \dot q_{\mathrm u})\) using the inverse Jacobian of \(c\).
  • \(\partial G_{\mathrm u}/\partial q_{\mathrm a}\) and \(\partial G_{\mathrm u}/\partial q_{\mathrm u}\) can be approximated by finite differences.
  • The loop produces references for the actuated coordinates; a low-level controller tracks them, for example \(\tau = G_{\mathrm a}(\bar q) + K_P(\bar q_{\mathrm a}-q_{\mathrm a}) - K_D\dot q_{\mathrm a}\). With \(K_P\) large enough, this works when the reference is a stable equilibrium (\(H_{\mathrm{uu}}\succ 0\)), and fails at an unstable one.
  • Start Algorithm 2 from a held or nearly held configuration, for example the robot's current one. Started on \(\mathcal{E}\), it stays on \(\mathcal{E}\) and skips the statics transient.

Algorithm 1 · statics solved beforehand

Walkthrough, about 80 s: each line of the pseudocode, its block in the scheme, and what it does on the running example; at the end, the same recipe for a two-segment soft arm.

define  q̄u(qa)          explicit equilibrium map
        f(qa) = h(c⁻¹(qa, q̄u(qa)))   actuators-to-task map (h∘ha)
        J(qa) = ∂f/∂qa = Ja − Ju Huu⁻¹Hua
choose  Γ ≻ 0, tolerance εx, step Δt, initial qa
while ‖x̄ − f(qa)‖ > εx
    ex ← x̄ − f(qa)
    qa ← qa + Δt · J(qa)⁻¹ Γ ex
    qu ← q̄u(qa)

Algorithm 2 · statics solved online

Walkthrough, about 1 min: the two corrections of each step, on the running example; at the end, the same recipe for a two-segment soft arm.

define  f(qa, qu) = h(c⁻¹(qa, qu))   task in actuation coordinates
        Ja, Ju       task Jacobians
        Hua, Huu     derivatives of Gu
choose  ΓZ, ΓE ≻ 0, tolerances, step Δt, initial (qa, qu)
while ‖x̄ − f‖ > εx  or  ‖Gu‖ > εE
    ex ← x̄ − f,   eE ← −Gu
    PE ← [I; −Huu⁻¹Hua] (I − Ja⁻¹Ju Huu⁻¹Hua)⁻¹
    PZ ← [−Ja⁻¹Ju; I] (I − Huu⁻¹Hua Ja⁻¹Ju)⁻¹
    (qa, qu) ← (qa, qu) + Δt · (PE Ja⁻¹ΓZ ex + PZ Huu⁻¹ΓE eE)

Abstract

Kinematic inversion via closed-loop schemes is at the core of the success of fully actuated robotic manipulators. These algorithms robustly and efficiently compute a reference for low-level controllers to position the end effector or other selected parts of the robot's body in space. This paper concerns itself with the extension of kinematic inversion algorithms beyond fully actuated systems into the realm of underactuated robotics. We propose two strategies that address this challenge under different assumptions. The first is obtained by revisiting a well-known kinematic inversion algorithm from continuum robotics, the direct task-to-actuators inversion, and reformulating it so that it generalizes beyond that field and beyond its current strong assumptions, including full actuation. We then introduce a second closed-loop inversion algorithm that applies in even more general conditions, no longer requiring explicit solutions of the robot's statics. This is achieved by showing that the configuration feasibility constraint can be expressed as a holonomic one and embedding it within the inverse kinematics loop in the manner of constrained CLIK.
